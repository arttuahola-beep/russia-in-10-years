Fuel limits spread as Putin promises retaliation, the 2027 budget clears the Duma and deaths outrun births by a record since covid

Refinery strikes push fuel limits into more regions and onto the Kremlin's agenda, the Duma passes the 2027 budget with about 17 trillion rubles for defence, inflation edges up to 7.08 per cent, a demographer estimates the largest natural loss since covid, the migrant-labour decree is still unsigned, Shenzhen ends with a declaration that does not name Ukraine, Europe is asked to Moscow rather than to the table, and the vision is unchanged.

What changed today

  • Strengthened. Fuel limits spread, and the Kremlin calls a meeting on them. Industry sources told Reuters that drone strikes in November had disrupted or stopped 15 of Russia's 35 largest refineries and cut their combined throughput by 45 per cent since late October, The Moscow Times reports. More regions have capped sales at the pump. Penza has limited drivers to 50 litres of petrol and 200 of diesel since 17 November; Sevastopol allows a full tank plus 20 litres in cans; Rostov says limits continue and hit independent stations most; earlier caps remain in Karelia, Buryatia, Zabaykalsky Krai, Leningrad, Nizhny Novgorod and Tomsk. On Monday Deputy Prime Minister Alexander Novak met oil companies about supplies in Oryol, Stavropol, Ingushetia and St Petersburg. Lukoil's refinery in Perm, one of the largest, was hit on 23 November and one person was killed. On Tuesday Vladimir Putin chaired the fuel and energy commission. He said petrol prices have stabilised since their October peak but the market "remains difficult", that Kyiv ignores President Donald Trump's request to stop the strikes, and that Russian retaliation is "inevitable". The budget also pays: subsidies to refiners under the damper mechanism and related payments may exceed 590 billion rubles in November, a record, according to a Reuters calculation. The vision says energy income can be hit at home as well as by price and sanctions abroad. The falsifier on a price-and-strike combination the budget cannot finance without a political crisis has not landed: the bill is large and the queues longer, but there is no crisis. (The Moscow Times, The Moscow Times, Interfax, Al Jazeera, with Reuters, Rossiyskaya Gazeta, The Moscow Times, citing Reuters)
  • Strengthened. Deaths outrun births by a record since covid. The demographer Alexei Raksha estimates that deaths exceeded births in Russia by 764,000 in 2025, with 1.167 million births, the lowest in the country's modern history, and 1.931 million deaths. For 2026 he puts the natural loss at no less than 820,000, about 2,200 people a day, The Moscow Times reports. Rosstat stopped publishing monthly births and deaths in the spring of 2025; the last official population figure is 146.12 million on 1 January 2025. The government's own 2023 forecast expected migration of about 200,000 a year to offset only 40 per cent of the natural loss to 2046. The vision says the population is smaller than in 2021 from low births, war deaths and emigration, that counts will be argued over, and that the direction is clear. A state that hides the count is part of the argument; the direction is the same. (The Moscow Times, MSK1.ru)
  • Does not move. The budget becomes law in the Duma. On 24 November the Duma passed the 2027–29 budget in its third reading: revenue of 43.3 trillion rubles, spending of 48.8 trillion and a deficit of 5.4 trillion, or 2.2 per cent of GDP, with about 17.15 trillion for national defence, RBC reports. It assumes a base oil price of $50 a barrel. Separate bills would add a 30 per cent tax on the excess profits of some extracting companies and widen the progressive income tax to passive income. Deputy Finance Minister Vladimir Kolychev said the budget's adaptation to recent shocks "is complete". Oil-and-gas revenue in November may be 45 per cent higher than a year earlier, but for January to November about 10 per cent lower, according to a Reuters calculation. The vision forecasts neither default nor easy fiscal room, a defence budget at wartime peaks and tax rises as signs of the squeeze. This is that budget, signed off. (RBC, State Duma, The Moscow Times, citing Reuters)
  • Does not move. Inflation edges up and the rate is expected to stay. Prices rose 0.19 per cent in the week to 16 November, after 0.12 per cent, and the Economy Ministry put annual inflation at 7.08 per cent; diesel rose 0.5 per cent in the week. Kirill Tremasov, an adviser to the central bank's governor, said inflation could have reached the target "near 4 per cent" this year were it not for the fuel market. Analysts at Finam, Gazprombank, Alfa-Bank and Freedom Global expect the key rate to stay at 14 per cent at the meeting on 18 December, Finmarket reports. The vision says the state is better at steering credit than at productivity and that consumption is weaker than a peacetime path. A high rate held up by the war's effect on fuel fits it. (Vedomosti, Finmarket, RBC)
  • Does not move. The migrant-labour decree is still unsigned, and the recruitment bill is still being drafted. No signature has been reported on the government's decree setting the share of foreign workers firms may employ in 2027, which would bar migrants from taxi work in eight regions. The Interior Ministry's bill on organised recruitment of foreign workers has not reached the Duma; Vedomosti reported this month that the ministry expects to submit it within two to three months, and the start of the scheme may move to 1 March 2027. Neither trigger set in September has fired. (Vedomosti, ppt.ru)
  • Does not move. Shenzhen: a declaration, and no word on Ukraine. Putin, Xi Jinping and Trump met for about an hour and twenty minutes on 19 November. Their declaration says a nuclear war cannot be won and must not be fought, calls for reform of the United Nations, promises to raise the supply of refined petroleum products and says the International Criminal Court must not "abuse" its jurisdiction. It does not mention the war in Ukraine. On Tuesday the Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said Putin handed no settlement proposals to either leader. The Brookings analyst Patricia Kim said Beijing has little interest in the risks of brokering a peace. Last week the chair left this item uncertain until it could judge what was said and done. The vision says China is the main economic partner, not an ally under orders, and keeps its own line on the war; a treaty alliance or a Chinese guarantee is outside the picture. Beijing hosted, set the agenda and promised nothing. The line holds. (The Guardian, The White House, Anadolu Agency)
  • Does not move. Europe is asked to Moscow, and Brussels answers with sanctions. In Shenzhen Putin said Moscow had invited European leaders to come to Moscow so their position could be "clarified", and only then would it decide the format of further talks; he also said military cooperation between Ukraine and Europe "in any form" is a problem. Peskov said on Tuesday that Russia still does not see what "added value" Europe would bring and that no date is set for the next round. The same day the European Commission proposed a 22nd sanctions package: transaction bans on about 60 Russian banks, more than 70 shadow-fleet tankers, a wider ban on energy services, including for refineries, and a ban on Russian former combatants entering Europe. The vision says contact continues and trust does not, and that the West is a lost market, not a closed file. Both halves had a week. (RFI, with AFP, Anadolu Agency, UPI)

The vision is unchanged and carried forward. Vision last revised: 30 September 2026. This edition covers the week since Wednesday 18 November. Two items strengthen the vision, five do not move it, none is uncertain and none weakens it. The ground-level stories lead again. Fuel limits have spread from a handful of regions to about a dozen, the Kremlin has made the refineries a presidential matter, and the budget is paying record subsidies to keep pump prices down. A demographer's count puts the natural loss of population at a level not seen since covid, while the state keeps its own figures secret. The chair asked whether the vision is now too thin on either. On fuel, the energy paragraph already names strikes at home, and the falsifier names a crisis the budget cannot finance; Putin says prices have stabilised, and no city has seen open protest. The migrant-labour trigger has not fired: if the 2027 decree is signed, which last year came in early December, or the recruitment bill is adopted, the vision will be revised loudly and Society line 8 rewritten. Last week's uncertain item, Shenzhen, is settled as not moving the text. Vision for 2036

This is a baseline, not a prophecy. It pictures the Russian Federation on 25 November 2036. The living baseline was last revised on 30 September 2026 and is carried forward. The horizon moves with the publication date: always ten years on. Nothing here is the necessary path of Russian history.

Political system and succession

Russia in 2036 is still a highly centralised state. Power sits in the presidency and the security services, behind managed elections — neither competitive democracy nor a formal one-party state. Who occupies the centre, and how much of the system is personal rather than institutional, stays open.

Vladimir Putin, born in 1952, would be 83 on this date. A continuing presidency, a managed handover, an elite bargain, or a sharper break after death or a loss of control are all open, and this text does not choose. Elites settle any transition in the central case before voters see it. Courts, governors, and the legislature are instruments of that centre more often than checks on it. A successor may inherit the offices without the same authority. Organised opposition stays repressed. A military setback, a fiscal squeeze, or a split in the security services could loosen control faster than any reform. Official talk of stability is not evidence that leaders can be changed without fear. A larger standing force and a heavier defence budget raise the stakes of any succession bargain; they do not settle who wins it.

Economy, technology, and sanctions

The economy has adapted to war without the openness of the 2010s or a collapse into autarky. Oil, gas, metals, agriculture, and the state sector still carry the budget. Consumption is weaker than a peacetime path. Military industry is larger and, on present plans, funded more heavily into 2027–29 than the opening baseline. Civilian technology depends on imports, copies, and partners outside Western export controls.

Sanctions still bind: thinner access to Western finance, advanced chips, machine tools, aircraft parts, and some energy equipment. Evasion and substitutes limit the damage without erasing it. A full return to Western finance and technology would still take a political settlement this baseline does not assume. Weekday news splits statute from practice: U.S. authorities to tighten further, including secondary tariffs on major buyers of Russian energy, now sit on the books, while early signals point to possible partial easing for prisoner deals before any Ukraine settlement. Secondary sanctions that China and India refuse to absorb would make the constraint harder. Neither automatic secondary tariffs nor an early broad relief package is the default. The state can steer credit to priority plants and is worse at productivity in ordinary firms. Artificial intelligence shows up as imported tools, uneven local models, and military and surveillance uses, not a broad productivity shift. Planned tax rises and National Wealth Fund draws are early signs of that squeeze. This text still forecasts neither a sovereign default nor easy fiscal room.

War and security

The war against Ukraine that began in 2022 still shapes security, whether active, frozen, or settled. This text does not choose an ending. A frozen line, a ceasefire neither side treats as final, a settlement that leaves Ukraine independent but constrained, and continued attrition all fit. Occupation of all of Ukraine does not, nor a collapse that removes Russia as a regional military power. Either is possible and would force a rewrite.

The attrition path through the late 2020s is firmer: authorised force levels keep rising on paper, defence spending is planned at wartime peaks, and diplomacy has restarted without closing the gaps on territory or alliances. The armed forces are larger and more experienced than in 2021, and worn. Drones, artillery, and air defence have raised the cost of a land war. Stockpiles are only partly rebuilt. Nuclear weapons remain the ultimate guarantee and a limit on how far others push. Use is not the base case; accident and misreading are less remote than in the 2010s. Belarus stays closely tied to Moscow, with its own succession open. The South Caucasus and Central Asia have a little more room to diversify partners, and less than they want. NATO’s eastern members spend and host more than before 2022.

Society and demography

The population is smaller than in 2021 from low births, war deaths and injuries, and emigration. Counts will be argued over; the direction is clear. Fewer young adults tighten the labour market and tempt the state to hold people with law, pay, and propaganda. A larger authorised force deepens that temptation. Many accommodate the leadership because leaving is costly and chaos is the advertised alternative; a smaller group does not. Polls under repression are weak evidence; emigration, enlistment, and births are better. Moscow and a few cities keep more skilled work. Regions that supply soldiers and raw materials do not automatically share the gains. Poor health and male mortality, worsened by the war, limit extraction and do not predict revolt.

Energy

Oil and gas still fund the state, for different customers. Pipeline gas to Europe is smaller than before 2022. China, India, Turkey, and others take more oil, often at a discount, on routes sanctions have not closed. New pipelines and LNG toward Asia have not replaced the old European volumes or prices. China buys and sets terms. Domestic power is gas, nuclear, hydro, and a slow remainder. Climate targets do not bind policy. Heat, fires, floods, and thawing Arctic ground cost more by the mid-2030s. Ukrainian long-range strikes on refining and related infrastructure, and tighter secrecy around refinery and export data, underline a vulnerability the opening baseline named only lightly: energy income can be hit at home as well as by price and sanctions abroad. A long stretch of cheap oil would hurt the budget more than slogans about sovereignty can repair.

China, the West, and the Global South

China is the main economic partner, not an ally under orders. Trade, some technology, and a shared interest in limiting American pressure hold the tie. A treaty alliance, a Chinese guarantee of the budget, and unlimited tolerance of secondary sanctions are outside this picture. Beijing keeps its own line on the war and on price. If the relationship is marked down, nothing of the same scale replaces it.

The United States, European governments, and the rest of the sanctions coalition are rivals and a lost market, not a closed file. Contact continues, including intermittent U.S. mediation attempts. Trust does not. Re-entry to Western finance and advanced technology still depends on the war and on politics in those capitals. A change of government can shift enforcement at more than the margin — including contested talk of pre-peace relief — and still cannot, alone, return trade to the 2000s. Arms, grain, and nuclear deals elsewhere win contracts. Few states organise their security around Moscow. India keeps other partners. Turkey hedges. They do not replace what was lost.

Russia in 2036 is a nuclear power, a large commodity exporter, and a regional military state living with a long war. It is strongest where it can bear costs and sell what others need, and weakest where productivity, demography, and partners who trust it decide the outcome.

How to read this revision

Philosophers

  • bot Pufendorf. A ruler who promises retaliation for a burning refinery has spoken of his enemy; the driver limited to thirty litres is asking what he owes his own people. A state that stops publishing how many of its citizens are born and die has not changed the fact, only its duty to tell it. A declaration that binds three powers to nothing has the form of a treaty without its obligation. The vision is unchanged.
  • bot Popper. Two items of seven fit the vision and five leave it where it was. Look at fuel and population. If pump limits reach Moscow and the state must cut other spending to pay the subsidies, and that brings open protest, the energy falsifier lands. If the refineries recover and the limits are lifted by the new year, the line holds as written. On population, the test is the falsifier already written: evidence that the working-age population has stabilised, or that losses are far larger than the baseline allows. A record natural loss is neither; it is the baseline, a little worse.
  • bot Socrates. If a country passes a budget that says its adaptation to shocks is complete, while its refineries burn, what has it adapted to? If the census is secret, who is it secret from? If three leaders sign a declaration on peace that does not name the war, what peace do they mean? And if Europe is invited to Moscow to explain itself, who thinks they are the examiner?

Falsifiers

  • A verified settlement or durable ceasefire that lets defence spending fall back toward pre-war shares of the budget.
  • A competitive transfer of power, or a loss of central authority across several regions, that the assumption of elite management cannot absorb.
  • A broad, durable lifting of Western sanctions and a return of advanced-technology trade, or a secondary-sanctions shock that cuts the main Asian energy outlets.
  • Evidence that the working-age population has stabilised, or that war deaths and emigration have been far larger than this baseline allows.
  • An energy system whose fiscal role has been replaced, or a price-and-strike combination that the budget cannot finance without a political crisis.
  • A binding Chinese security guarantee, or a Chinese decision to curtail the economic relationship enough to remove it as the main external pillar.

Society in ten points

A compact picture of Russian Federation society at the horizon. The ten labels stay fixed. Rewrite a line only when the vision itself has a material social change — not when the day’s news only deepens an already-named path.

  1. Form of government — Highly centralised presidential–security state with managed elections; neither competitive democracy nor a formal one-party system.
  2. Social trust — Low generalised trust: many accommodate because exit is costly and chaos is the advertised alternative, not because institutions earn confidence.
  3. Type of economy — War-adapted commodity and state-led economy; oil, gas, metals, agriculture and military industry carry the budget; civilian technology leans on imports, copies and non-Western partners.
  4. Freedom of speech and press — Narrow and managed; independent journalism and open dissent are repressed; the official narrative dominates public channels.
  5. Rule of law — Instrumental: courts, governors and the legislature serve the centre more often than they check it.
  6. Political competition — Organised opposition stays repressed; succession and elite bargains settle before voters see them.
  7. Civil society — Independent association is thin and risky outside state-approved forms.
  8. Demography and social fabric — Smaller population than 2021 from low births, war deaths and emigration; fewer young adults tighten labour; Moscow and a few cities keep more skilled work than regions that supply soldiers and raw materials.
  9. Information and surveillance — Strong state information control and surveillance, including military and monitoring uses of technology.
  10. Security apparatus in society — Security services and a large armed force sit at the centre of political power; a long-war footing deepens that role through the late 2020s.
