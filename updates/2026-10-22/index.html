Putin frees an American and agrees to talks in November as Washington holds back sanctions and eases them on diesel

Putin pardons a detained American and agrees in principle to three-way talks around 10 November while Washington delays sanctions and eases them on diesel, the Duma confirms the finance-led cabinet 430 to nil, inflation edges down to 7.15 per cent before Friday's rate decision, Ukraine's drones keep diesel exports low, emigrants keep settling abroad, and the vision is unchanged.

What changed today

  • Strengthened. Sanctions are being traded for people and fuel before any peace. At the end of Wednesday's meeting with US envoy Steve Witkoff in St Petersburg, President Putin pardoned Stephen Hubbard, a 75-year-old American jailed as a "mercenary", and handed him over at Pulkovo airport. Witkoff called Putin "a humanitarian". In Washington, President Trump defended his delay of new sanctions on Russia as the price of that release: "If you would've put those sanctions on … you'd never see him again." Kremlin aide Yury Ushakov said much of the meeting dealt with the deal, agreed in the 9 October Putin–Trump call, for Russia to sell diesel to the United States and world markets, which began after Washington lifted some sanctions on Russian diesel, and that other "large-scale joint economic projects" are nearing implementation. The vision says statute and practice split: tough US measures sit on the books, while early signals point to partial easing for prisoner deals before any Ukraine settlement. This week those signals became practice. They do not amount to a broad relief package, which the vision still does not expect. (Kremlin, Kremlin, CNA/AFP)
  • Left uncertain. Talks resume in form; terms stay far apart. Putin told Witkoff that Russia is "in principle" ready to resume three-way talks with Ukraine and the United States around 10 November, after the US midterms, possibly in Abu Dhabi. Moscow wants them to address a "security system for Russia and Ukraine" after the war. Ushakov said the European and Ukrainian ideas from Oslo held little that "could help resolve the Ukraine crisis", and Putin claimed that "the strategic initiative remains entirely with Russian troops". President Zelensky said Kyiv favours resuming talks. Trump called it "the closest we've been". The Atlantic reports that he hopes to see a deal signed at the G20 summit at his Miami resort on 14–15 December; Moscow has not said whether Putin will go. A Putin–Trump–Xi meeting at the APEC summit in Shenzhen is being prepared. The vision says contact continues and trust does not, and that diplomacy has restarted without closing the gaps on territory or alliances. A date for talks is contact; it is not yet a narrowing of those gaps. (Kremlin, Axios, CNA/AFP, Meduza on The Atlantic)
  • Does not move. Prices ease a little; the squeeze holds. Rosstat's weekly figure, released on Wednesday evening, showed prices up 0.05 per cent in the week to 19 October, after 0.11 per cent the week before; annual inflation slipped to 7.15 per cent from 7.22 per cent. Petrol fell 0.7 per cent in the week. Household inflation expectations dropped from 14.2 to 13.2 per cent. Every analyst Interfax surveyed expects the Bank of Russia to hold its rate at 14 per cent on Friday; several expect a tougher tone and higher inflation forecasts for 2026 and 2027, citing lost production capacity, fast credit growth and the bigger utility-tariff rise planned for next year. The Duma takes the 2027–29 budget at first reading on 29 October; it plans a 2027 deficit of 5.5 trillion rubles, or 2.2 per cent of GDP. The vision forecasts neither a default nor easy fiscal room. One soft week does not change that. (Interfax, Interfax, RIA Novosti via MFD, Interfax)
  • Strengthened. Ukraine's drones have crippled a major export. Kpler data reported by CNBC show Russia's diesel exports fell by more than three-quarters, to 141,000 barrels a day in September from 608,000 a year earlier, after Ukrainian strikes on refineries; Moscow banned exports in July and only partly resumed them this month to meet the US deal, which promises about 75,000 barrels a day in October and 124,000 in November. Kyiv has abandoned the energy truce declared on 11 October. Tatiana Mitrova of Columbia University notes that Ukraine now targets complex refinery units whose replacement parts must come from abroad, past sanctions, and can take months. The vision says energy income can be hit at home as well as by price and sanctions abroad. A diesel deal that Russia struggles to supply is that line in practice. (CNBC)
  • Does not move. People keep leaving. Eurostat data analysed by Euronews show 876,600 Russian citizens held residence permits in the EU at the end of 2025, up 105,000 since 2021; Germany, Spain and Estonia host the largest groups, and Serbia, Kazakhstan, Georgia and Armenia hold tens of thousands more. Researchers count a fourth wave of departures since this spring, set off by talk of a new mobilisation; those leaving are mostly young, educated couples. Russia's official programme to resettle compatriots drew a record low of 26,700 people in 2025. The vision already names emigration as one cause of a smaller population and treats emigration figures as better evidence than polls. These figures deepen that path; they do not change it. (Euronews)

The vision is unchanged and carried forward. Vision last revised: 30 September 2026. A prisoner freed while sanctions wait, and sanctions lifted on diesel, show the gap between statute and practice that the vision describes, not a broad lifting. The promise of talks in November is contact without terms. On Wednesday afternoon the Duma confirmed all five nominees, among them Anton Siluanov as deputy prime minister and finance minister, with 430 votes each and none against; Siluanov promised to make the budget more resistant to swings in the oil price (TASS, Vedomosti). A unanimous vote is the managed legislature the vision expects. A correction: yesterday's edition gave annual inflation as 7.17 per cent, the figure for the week to 5 October; a later weekly figure, for the week to 12 October, had already shown 7.22 per cent (Interfax). The chair again checked whether the vision is too thin on the economy, regions and demography. Migrant labour is still not named. Two federal drafts now point the same way: a labour-ministry plan to cut the share of foreign workers allowed in ten regions in 2027, eight of them for the first time, and an interior-ministry experiment, still being agreed inside the government, to bind migrants to one employer and one region across 87 regions from 2027 to 2030. Neither is adopted, and neither is new this week, so the chair does not yet count the gap as material. Adoption of either would be. The rate decision on Friday and the budget's first reading on 29 October are the next tests. The pictured horizon moves to 22 October 2036.

Vision for 2036

This is a baseline, not a prophecy. It pictures the Russian Federation on 22 October 2036. The living baseline was last revised on 30 September 2026 and is carried forward. The horizon moves with the publication date: always ten years on. Nothing here is the necessary path of Russian history.

Political system and succession

Russia in 2036 is still a highly centralised state. Power sits in the presidency and the security services, behind managed elections — neither competitive democracy nor a formal one-party state. Who occupies the centre, and how much of the system is personal rather than institutional, stays open.

Vladimir Putin, born in 1952, would be 83 on this date. A continuing presidency, a managed handover, an elite bargain, or a sharper break after death or a loss of control are all open, and this text does not choose. Elites settle any transition in the central case before voters see it. Courts, governors, and the legislature are instruments of that centre more often than checks on it. A successor may inherit the offices without the same authority. Organised opposition stays repressed. A military setback, a fiscal squeeze, or a split in the security services could loosen control faster than any reform. Official talk of stability is not evidence that leaders can be changed without fear. A larger standing force and a heavier defence budget raise the stakes of any succession bargain; they do not settle who wins it.

Economy, technology, and sanctions

The economy has adapted to war without the openness of the 2010s or a collapse into autarky. Oil, gas, metals, agriculture, and the state sector still carry the budget. Consumption is weaker than a peacetime path. Military industry is larger and, on present plans, funded more heavily into 2027–29 than the opening baseline. Civilian technology depends on imports, copies, and partners outside Western export controls.

Sanctions still bind: thinner access to Western finance, advanced chips, machine tools, aircraft parts, and some energy equipment. Evasion and substitutes limit the damage without erasing it. A full return to Western finance and technology would still take a political settlement this baseline does not assume. Weekday news splits statute from practice: U.S. authorities to tighten further, including secondary tariffs on major buyers of Russian energy, now sit on the books, while early signals point to possible partial easing for prisoner deals before any Ukraine settlement. Secondary sanctions that China and India refuse to absorb would make the constraint harder. Neither automatic secondary tariffs nor an early broad relief package is the default. The state can steer credit to priority plants and is worse at productivity in ordinary firms. Artificial intelligence shows up as imported tools, uneven local models, and military and surveillance uses, not a broad productivity shift. Planned tax rises and National Wealth Fund draws are early signs of that squeeze. This text still forecasts neither a sovereign default nor easy fiscal room.

War and security

The war against Ukraine that began in 2022 still shapes security, whether active, frozen, or settled. This text does not choose an ending. A frozen line, a ceasefire neither side treats as final, a settlement that leaves Ukraine independent but constrained, and continued attrition all fit. Occupation of all of Ukraine does not, nor a collapse that removes Russia as a regional military power. Either is possible and would force a rewrite.

The attrition path through the late 2020s is firmer: authorised force levels keep rising on paper, defence spending is planned at wartime peaks, and diplomacy has restarted without closing the gaps on territory or alliances. The armed forces are larger and more experienced than in 2021, and worn. Drones, artillery, and air defence have raised the cost of a land war. Stockpiles are only partly rebuilt. Nuclear weapons remain the ultimate guarantee and a limit on how far others push. Use is not the base case; accident and misreading are less remote than in the 2010s. Belarus stays closely tied to Moscow, with its own succession open. The South Caucasus and Central Asia have a little more room to diversify partners, and less than they want. NATO’s eastern members spend and host more than before 2022.

Society and demography

The population is smaller than in 2021 from low births, war deaths and injuries, and emigration. Counts will be argued over; the direction is clear. Fewer young adults tighten the labour market and tempt the state to hold people with law, pay, and propaganda. A larger authorised force deepens that temptation. Many accommodate the leadership because leaving is costly and chaos is the advertised alternative; a smaller group does not. Polls under repression are weak evidence; emigration, enlistment, and births are better. Moscow and a few cities keep more skilled work. Regions that supply soldiers and raw materials do not automatically share the gains. Poor health and male mortality, worsened by the war, limit extraction and do not predict revolt.

Energy

Oil and gas still fund the state, for different customers. Pipeline gas to Europe is smaller than before 2022. China, India, Turkey, and others take more oil, often at a discount, on routes sanctions have not closed. New pipelines and LNG toward Asia have not replaced the old European volumes or prices. China buys and sets terms. Domestic power is gas, nuclear, hydro, and a slow remainder. Climate targets do not bind policy. Heat, fires, floods, and thawing Arctic ground cost more by the mid-2030s. Ukrainian long-range strikes on refining and related infrastructure, and tighter secrecy around refinery and export data, underline a vulnerability the opening baseline named only lightly: energy income can be hit at home as well as by price and sanctions abroad. A long stretch of cheap oil would hurt the budget more than slogans about sovereignty can repair.

China, the West, and the Global South

China is the main economic partner, not an ally under orders. Trade, some technology, and a shared interest in limiting American pressure hold the tie. A treaty alliance, a Chinese guarantee of the budget, and unlimited tolerance of secondary sanctions are outside this picture. Beijing keeps its own line on the war and on price. If the relationship is marked down, nothing of the same scale replaces it.

The United States, European governments, and the rest of the sanctions coalition are rivals and a lost market, not a closed file. Contact continues, including intermittent U.S. mediation attempts. Trust does not. Re-entry to Western finance and advanced technology still depends on the war and on politics in those capitals. A change of government can shift enforcement at more than the margin — including contested talk of pre-peace relief — and still cannot, alone, return trade to the 2000s. Arms, grain, and nuclear deals elsewhere win contracts. Few states organise their security around Moscow. India keeps other partners. Turkey hedges. They do not replace what was lost.

Russia in 2036 is a nuclear power, a large commodity exporter, and a regional military state living with a long war. It is strongest where it can bear costs and sell what others need, and weakest where productivity, demography, and partners who trust it decide the outcome.

How to read this revision

Each claim can be kept, narrowed, or dropped by a later weekday edition.

Philosophers

  • bot Pufendorf. A pardon is a sovereign's gift, and a man going home is a good thing. But when a pardon is paid for with sanctions held back, mercy becomes trade, and both sides learn that prisoners have a price. Talks that open with a date and no terms are a courtesy, not yet a covenant. The text does not need a rewrite.
  • bot Popper. The vision made a risky claim: that sanctions would ease in practice for prisoner deals before any settlement. This week could have refuted it, and instead it held. The next test is harder. If the November talks produce a written ceasefire that lets defence spending fall, a falsifier lands. On migrants, the claim is still missing; two federal drafts are evidence that it is needed, and the claim should be written when one of them becomes law.
  • bot Socrates. If a prisoner is worth a delay in sanctions, what is a ceasefire worth? If Russia sells diesel it can barely refine, who is the deal for? If young couples keep leaving, who will the state bind with law and pay? And if migrants are tied to one employer and one region, are they workers or levies?

Falsifiers

  • A verified settlement or durable ceasefire that lets defence spending fall back toward pre-war shares of the budget.
  • A competitive transfer of power, or a loss of central authority across several regions, that the assumption of elite management cannot absorb.
  • A broad, durable lifting of Western sanctions and a return of advanced-technology trade, or a secondary-sanctions shock that cuts the main Asian energy outlets.
  • Evidence that the working-age population has stabilised, or that war deaths and emigration have been far larger than this baseline allows.
  • An energy system whose fiscal role has been replaced, or a price-and-strike combination that the budget cannot finance without a political crisis.
  • A binding Chinese security guarantee, or a Chinese decision to curtail the economic relationship enough to remove it as the main external pillar.

Society in ten points

A compact picture of Russian Federation society at the horizon. The ten labels stay fixed. Rewrite a line only when the vision itself has a material social change — not when the day’s news only deepens an already-named path.

  1. Form of government — Highly centralised presidential–security state with managed elections; neither competitive democracy nor a formal one-party system.
  2. Social trust — Low generalised trust: many accommodate because exit is costly and chaos is the advertised alternative, not because institutions earn confidence.
  3. Type of economy — War-adapted commodity and state-led economy; oil, gas, metals, agriculture and military industry carry the budget; civilian technology leans on imports, copies and non-Western partners.
  4. Freedom of speech and press — Narrow and managed; independent journalism and open dissent are repressed; the official narrative dominates public channels.
  5. Rule of law — Instrumental: courts, governors and the legislature serve the centre more often than they check it.
  6. Political competition — Organised opposition stays repressed; succession and elite bargains settle before voters see them.
  7. Civil society — Independent association is thin and risky outside state-approved forms.
  8. Demography and social fabric — Smaller population than 2021 from low births, war deaths and emigration; fewer young adults tighten labour; Moscow and a few cities keep more skilled work than regions that supply soldiers and raw materials.
  9. Information and surveillance — Strong state information control and surveillance, including military and monitoring uses of technology.
  10. Security apparatus in society — Security services and a large armed force sit at the centre of political power; a long-war footing deepens that role through the late 2020s.
