## Vision for 2036

This is a baseline, not a prophecy. It pictures the Russian Federation on 29 September 2036. Later editions move the horizon with them: always ten years on, never a fixed end year. Nothing here is the necessary path of Russian history.

### Political system and succession

Russia in 2036 is still a highly centralised state. Power sits in the presidency and the security services, behind managed elections. This baseline assumes neither competitive democracy nor a formal one-party state. The open question is who occupies the centre, and how much of the system is personal rather than institutional.

Vladimir Putin was born in 1952. On this date he would be 83. A continuing presidency, a managed handover, an elite bargain, or a sharper break after death or a loss of control are all open, and this text does not choose. Any transition in the central case is settled among elites before voters see it. Courts, governors, and the legislature are instruments of that centre more often than checks on it. A successor may inherit the offices without the same authority. Repression of organised opposition continues. A military setback, a fiscal squeeze, or a split in the security services could loosen control faster than any reform. Official talk of stability is not evidence that leaders can be changed without fear.

### Economy, technology, and sanctions

The economy has adapted to war without returning to the openness of the 2010s or collapsing into autarky. Oil, gas, metals, agriculture, and the state sector still carry the budget. Consumption is weaker than a peacetime path, and military industry is larger. Civilian technology depends on imports, copies, and partners outside Western export controls.

Sanctions still bind: thinner access to Western finance, advanced chips, machine tools, aircraft parts, and some energy equipment. Evasion and substitutes limit the damage without erasing it. Lifting them would take a political settlement this baseline does not assume. Secondary sanctions that China and India refuse to absorb would make the constraint harder. Neither is the default. The state can steer credit to priority plants and is worse at productivity in ordinary firms. Artificial intelligence shows up as imported tools, uneven local models, and military and surveillance uses, not as a broad productivity shift. The budget remains sensitive to the oil price and to the cost of the war. This text forecasts neither a sovereign default nor easy fiscal room.

### War and security

The war against Ukraine that began in 2022 still shapes security, whether it is then active, frozen, or settled. This text does not choose an ending. A frozen line, a ceasefire neither side treats as final, a settlement that leaves Ukraine independent but constrained, and continued attrition all fit the rest of the baseline. Occupation of all of Ukraine does not. Nor does a collapse that removes Russia as a regional military power. Either is possible, and either would force a rewrite.

The armed forces are larger and more experienced than in 2021, and worn. Drones, artillery, and air defence have raised the cost of a land war. Stockpiles are only partly rebuilt. Nuclear weapons remain the ultimate guarantee and a limit on how far others push. Their use is not the base case, though accident and misreading are less remote than in the 2010s. Belarus stays closely tied to Moscow, with its own succession open. The South Caucasus and Central Asia have a little more room to diversify partners, and less than they want. NATO's eastern members spend and host more than before 2022.

### Society and demography

The population is smaller than in 2021 because of low births, war deaths and injuries, and emigration. Counts will be argued over. The direction is clear. Fewer young adults tighten the labour market and tempt the state to hold people with law, pay, and propaganda. Many accommodate the leadership because leaving is costly and chaos is the advertised alternative. A smaller group does not. Polls under repression are weak evidence; emigration, enlistment, and births are better. Moscow and a few cities keep more of the skilled work. Regions that supply soldiers and raw materials do not automatically share the gains. Poor health and male mortality, worsened by the war, limit how much the state can extract. They are not a prediction of revolt.

### Energy

Oil and gas still fund the state, for a different set of customers. Pipeline gas to Europe is a much smaller business than before 2022. China, India, Turkey, and others take more oil, often at a discount, along routes sanctions have not closed. New pipelines and LNG toward Asia have not replaced the old European volumes or prices. China buys and sets terms. Domestic power is gas, nuclear, hydro, and a slow remainder. Climate targets do not bind policy here. Heat, fires, floods, and thawing Arctic ground do cost more by the mid-2030s. A long period of cheap oil would hurt the budget more than slogans about sovereignty can repair.

### China, the West, and the Global South

China is the main economic partner and not an ally under orders. Trade, some technology, and a shared interest in limiting American pressure hold the tie. A treaty alliance, a Chinese guarantee of the budget, and unlimited tolerance of secondary sanctions are outside this picture. Beijing keeps its own line on the war and on price. If the relationship is marked down, there is no replacement of the same scale.

The United States, European governments, and the rest of the sanctions coalition are rivals and a lost market, not a closed file. Contact of some kind continues. Trust does not. Re-entry to Western finance and advanced technology depends on the war and on politics in those capitals, which a change of government can shift at the margin and cannot, alone, return to the 2000s. Arms, grain, and nuclear deals elsewhere win contracts. Few states organise their security around Moscow. India keeps other partners. Turkey hedges. The transactions are real. They do not replace what was lost.

Russia in 2036 is a nuclear power, a large commodity exporter, and a regional military state living with a long war. It is strongest where it can bear costs and sell what others need, and weakest where productivity, demography, and partners who trust it decide the outcome.

### How to read this baseline

Each claim can be kept, narrowed, or dropped by a later weekday edition. If the page starts to sound like fate, it has failed.
