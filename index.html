Inflation passes 7 per cent and state borrowing costs climb as a US envoy meets Putin in St Petersburg

A delayed utility-tariff rise lifts inflation above 7 per cent as state borrowing costs and the deficit climb, a US envoy meets Putin after talks in Oslo, Ukraine's drones reach pipelines and data centres, the front stalls, St Petersburg pushes migrant workers out of shops, and the vision is unchanged.

What changed today

  • Strengthened. The fiscal squeeze now shows in prices and in the bond market. Annual inflation passed 7 per cent, reaching 7.17 per cent in the week to 5 October, after the government raised household utility tariffs on 1 October, a rise held back from its usual July date until after the Duma elections. Bank of Russia economists say underlying inflation is still "markedly above" target. The Finance Ministry cancelled one bond auction for lack of acceptable bids and paid 16.77 per cent at another on 14 October, the highest since February 2025. It now expects a 2026 federal deficit of 7.3 trillion rubles, or 3.2 per cent of GDP, against an original plan of 3.8 trillion, because spending is running 4.5 trillion above plan and oil and gas revenue has fallen. Samolet, the largest housebuilder, went into technical default on a bond, and corporate profits are down 16 per cent. The central bank is expected to hold its rate at 14 per cent on Friday 23 October; the next Rosstat weekly price figure is due this evening. The vision says tax rises and National Wealth Fund draws are early signs of a squeeze, and forecasts neither a default nor easy fiscal room. Higher prices, dearer debt and a wider deficit fit that line closely. (bne IntelliNews, Kommersant)
  • Left uncertain. Talks resume; terms do not. US envoy Steve Witkoff is in St Petersburg to meet Putin today, a day after talks in Oslo with President Zelensky and the leaders of Finland and Norway that a US official called "very productive and constructive". Foreign Minister Lavrov said Moscow will listen but ruled out "any political concessions". Kremlin spokesman Peskov said a final settlement is impossible without someone from Kyiv "prepared to take on the responsibility". President Trump says he is ready to meet Putin and Xi at the APEC summit in Shenzhen on 18–19 November, mainly about nuclear weapons, and told Ukraine it is "time to end this war". Security Council deputy chairman Dmitry Medvedev wrote that Russia and the United States are and will remain "systemic adversaries". The vision says contact continues and trust does not, and that diplomacy has restarted without closing the gaps on territory or alliances. Today's meeting will test that; nothing is agreed. (BBC, Euronews, ISW)
  • Strengthened. Ukraine keeps hitting Russia's economy at home. Overnight into Tuesday, drones set fire to a Transneft pumping station on the Druzhba pipeline system in the Tambov region, about 340 kilometres from the border; the regional governor confirmed that a business in the district was struck. Earlier this month drones hit Yandex, Sberbank and VK data centres. The online-retail lobby AKIT has asked Prime Minister Mishustin for emergency rules that would let firms keep trading when state data systems fail, and even store Russians' personal data in "friendly" countries, Kommersant reported. In Washington, Vice-President Vance said Ukraine should stop bombing Russian energy. The vision says energy income can be hit at home as well as by price and sanctions abroad. The strikes now reach data as well as fuel. (ISW, Euromaidan Press, BBC)
  • Does not move. The front. The Institute for the Study of War assesses that Russian forces lost a net 122 square kilometres between 1 September and 16 October, after a Ukrainian counter-attack near Lyman pushed them back to the Zherebets River, and that Russia has gained only about 100 square kilometres since January. It says contract recruitment keeps getting more expensive. Russian forces still press near Pokrovsk and Orikhiv, and Russia launched 165 drones at Ukraine's energy sites overnight into Tuesday. These are one research group's estimates of a contested war. The vision does not choose an ending; it expects attrition through the late 2020s, with forces larger, more experienced and worn. A stalled offensive is attrition, not a turn. (ISW)
  • Left uncertain. Migrant labour, at ground level. St Petersburg has barred foreign workers on labour patents from jobs in shops, salons, health care and gyms until the end of the year, on top of an earlier ban on taxi and courier work; citizens of Eurasian Economic Union states are exempt. City officials call it a fight against off-the-books work. On 16 October police and riot police checked papers at the Sennoy market, and men who had recently become Russian citizens were handed military call-up papers, Fontanka reported. Since the 2024 Crocus City Hall attack, Russia has made residence harder to obtain and expulsion easier. The vision says fewer young adults tighten the labour market and tempt the state to hold people with law, pay and propaganda. It does not say whether Russia will fill that gap with migrants or push them out. St Petersburg is pushing them out, and drafting some of those who stay. (Meduza, Fontanka)

Vision for Tuesday 21 October 2036

Vision for 2036

This is a baseline, not a prophecy. It pictures the Russian Federation on 21 October 2036. The living baseline was last revised on 30 September 2026 and is carried forward. The horizon moves with the publication date: always ten years on. Nothing here is the necessary path of Russian history.

Political system and succession

Russia in 2036 is still a highly centralised state. Power sits in the presidency and the security services, behind managed elections — neither competitive democracy nor a formal one-party state. Who occupies the centre, and how much of the system is personal rather than institutional, stays open.

Vladimir Putin, born in 1952, would be 83 on this date. A continuing presidency, a managed handover, an elite bargain, or a sharper break after death or a loss of control are all open, and this text does not choose. Elites settle any transition in the central case before voters see it. Courts, governors, and the legislature are instruments of that centre more often than checks on it. A successor may inherit the offices without the same authority. Organised opposition stays repressed. A military setback, a fiscal squeeze, or a split in the security services could loosen control faster than any reform. Official talk of stability is not evidence that leaders can be changed without fear. A larger standing force and a heavier defence budget raise the stakes of any succession bargain; they do not settle who wins it.

Economy, technology, and sanctions

The economy has adapted to war without the openness of the 2010s or a collapse into autarky. Oil, gas, metals, agriculture, and the state sector still carry the budget. Consumption is weaker than a peacetime path. Military industry is larger and, on present plans, funded more heavily into 2027–29 than the opening baseline. Civilian technology depends on imports, copies, and partners outside Western export controls.

Sanctions still bind: thinner access to Western finance, advanced chips, machine tools, aircraft parts, and some energy equipment. Evasion and substitutes limit the damage without erasing it. A full return to Western finance and technology would still take a political settlement this baseline does not assume. Weekday news splits statute from practice: U.S. authorities to tighten further, including secondary tariffs on major buyers of Russian energy, now sit on the books, while early signals point to possible partial easing for prisoner deals before any Ukraine settlement. Secondary sanctions that China and India refuse to absorb would make the constraint harder. Neither automatic secondary tariffs nor an early broad relief package is the default. The state can steer credit to priority plants and is worse at productivity in ordinary firms. Artificial intelligence shows up as imported tools, uneven local models, and military and surveillance uses, not a broad productivity shift. Planned tax rises and National Wealth Fund draws are early signs of that squeeze. This text still forecasts neither a sovereign default nor easy fiscal room.

War and security

The war against Ukraine that began in 2022 still shapes security, whether active, frozen, or settled. This text does not choose an ending. A frozen line, a ceasefire neither side treats as final, a settlement that leaves Ukraine independent but constrained, and continued attrition all fit. Occupation of all of Ukraine does not, nor a collapse that removes Russia as a regional military power. Either is possible and would force a rewrite.

The attrition path through the late 2020s is firmer: authorised force levels keep rising on paper, defence spending is planned at wartime peaks, and diplomacy has restarted without closing the gaps on territory or alliances. The armed forces are larger and more experienced than in 2021, and worn. Drones, artillery, and air defence have raised the cost of a land war. Stockpiles are only partly rebuilt. Nuclear weapons remain the ultimate guarantee and a limit on how far others push. Use is not the base case; accident and misreading are less remote than in the 2010s. Belarus stays closely tied to Moscow, with its own succession open. The South Caucasus and Central Asia have a little more room to diversify partners, and less than they want. NATO’s eastern members spend and host more than before 2022.

Society and demography

The population is smaller than in 2021 from low births, war deaths and injuries, and emigration. Counts will be argued over; the direction is clear. Fewer young adults tighten the labour market and tempt the state to hold people with law, pay, and propaganda. A larger authorised force deepens that temptation. Many accommodate the leadership because leaving is costly and chaos is the advertised alternative; a smaller group does not. Polls under repression are weak evidence; emigration, enlistment, and births are better. Moscow and a few cities keep more skilled work. Regions that supply soldiers and raw materials do not automatically share the gains. Poor health and male mortality, worsened by the war, limit extraction and do not predict revolt.

Energy

Oil and gas still fund the state, for different customers. Pipeline gas to Europe is smaller than before 2022. China, India, Turkey, and others take more oil, often at a discount, on routes sanctions have not closed. New pipelines and LNG toward Asia have not replaced the old European volumes or prices. China buys and sets terms. Domestic power is gas, nuclear, hydro, and a slow remainder. Climate targets do not bind policy. Heat, fires, floods, and thawing Arctic ground cost more by the mid-2030s. Ukrainian long-range strikes on refining and related infrastructure, and tighter secrecy around refinery and export data, underline a vulnerability the opening baseline named only lightly: energy income can be hit at home as well as by price and sanctions abroad. A long stretch of cheap oil would hurt the budget more than slogans about sovereignty can repair.

China, the West, and the Global South

China is the main economic partner, not an ally under orders. Trade, some technology, and a shared interest in limiting American pressure hold the tie. A treaty alliance, a Chinese guarantee of the budget, and unlimited tolerance of secondary sanctions are outside this picture. Beijing keeps its own line on the war and on price. If the relationship is marked down, nothing of the same scale replaces it.

The United States, European governments, and the rest of the sanctions coalition are rivals and a lost market, not a closed file. Contact continues, including intermittent U.S. mediation attempts. Trust does not. Re-entry to Western finance and advanced technology still depends on the war and on politics in those capitals. A change of government can shift enforcement at more than the margin — including contested talk of pre-peace relief — and still cannot, alone, return trade to the 2000s. Arms, grain, and nuclear deals elsewhere win contracts. Few states organise their security around Moscow. India keeps other partners. Turkey hedges. They do not replace what was lost.

Russia in 2036 is a nuclear power, a large commodity exporter, and a regional military state living with a long war. It is strongest where it can bear costs and sell what others need, and weakest where productivity, demography, and partners who trust it decide the outcome.

Society in ten points

A compact picture of Russian Federation society at the horizon. The ten labels stay fixed. Rewrite a line only when the vision itself has a material social change — not when the day’s news only deepens an already-named path.

  1. Form of government — Highly centralised presidential–security state with managed elections; neither competitive democracy nor a formal one-party system.
  2. Social trust — Low generalised trust: many accommodate because exit is costly and chaos is the advertised alternative, not because institutions earn confidence.
  3. Type of economy — War-adapted commodity and state-led economy; oil, gas, metals, agriculture and military industry carry the budget; civilian technology leans on imports, copies and non-Western partners.
  4. Freedom of speech and press — Narrow and managed; independent journalism and open dissent are repressed; the official narrative dominates public channels.
  5. Rule of law — Instrumental: courts, governors and the legislature serve the centre more often than they check it.
  6. Political competition — Organised opposition stays repressed; succession and elite bargains settle before voters see them.
  7. Civil society — Independent association is thin and risky outside state-approved forms.
  8. Demography and social fabric — Smaller population than 2021 from low births, war deaths and emigration; fewer young adults tighten labour; Moscow and a few cities keep more skilled work than regions that supply soldiers and raw materials.
  9. Information and surveillance — Strong state information control and surveillance, including military and monitoring uses of technology.
  10. Security apparatus in society — Security services and a large armed force sit at the centre of political power; a long-war footing deepens that role through the late 2020s.
